# Anti-slop Patterns

These are suspicion triggers, not bans. Apply judgment from the rendered result.

## Composition defaults

### The centered-stack reflex

Symptoms:

- eyebrow pill
- huge centered headline
- centered subtitle
- two centered CTA buttons
- glowing orb
- three cards below

Why it often feels generated: it is a high-probability layout that requires almost no product-specific composition decision.

Possible fixes: introduce meaningful asymmetry, product imagery with a real role, stronger left/right tension, or task-specific structure.

### The three-card reflex

Symptoms: every concept becomes exactly three equal cards with icon, title, sentence.

Why it fails: equal boxes imply equal meaning and flatten the content hierarchy.

Possible fixes: let the content shape the layout; use one dominant idea plus supporting evidence, a timeline, a comparison, a real workflow, or a denser list where appropriate.

### Box soup

Symptoms: every group has background + border + radius + shadow.

Why it fails: grouping is outsourced to chrome instead of spacing, alignment, and typography.

Possible fixes: remove containers selectively, merge related groups, vary surface strength based on semantic importance.

## Decoration defaults

### Gradient inflation

Suspicious when gradients appear simultaneously in headline text, CTA, backgrounds, borders, and icons.

A gradient is fine when it has a clear brand or depth role. It becomes slop when it is the default way to make something “premium.”

### Glow-as-quality

Large blurred blobs behind content often simulate polish without improving structure.

Keep them only if the composition still feels intentional without them.

### Radius monoculture

If everything is rounded to the same generous radius—buttons, cards, screenshots, badges, inputs, nav—it can make the interface feel toy-like and generic.

Use shape hierarchy deliberately.

### Shadow monoculture

Repeated soft shadows on every surface destroy depth hierarchy.

Reserve elevation for surfaces that are actually above others.

## Typography defaults

### Semibold everywhere

When every label, stat, nav item, card title, and button is semibold, weight stops encoding hierarchy.

### Giant headline as substitute for composition

Large type can be powerful, but 72–96px alone does not make a hero intentional.

### Generic marketing language

Suspicious phrases include formulations like:

- “Transform your workflow”
- “Unlock the power of …”
- “Supercharge your …”
- “Built for the future”
- “Everything you need to …”
- “Smarter. Faster. Better.”

Do not rewrite copy unless the user allows it. If copy is generic, note how it weakens visual specificity.

## Interaction defaults

### Two equal CTAs

Primary and secondary actions should rarely compete at identical weight.

### Decorative icon spam

Icons should support recognition or action. Random sparkles, arrows, lightning bolts, and magic-wand glyphs often make AI tools visually interchangeable.

### Fake data theater

Avoid decorative dashboards and invented metrics that exist only to make a screenshot feel “product-like.” Prefer real or clearly labeled sample content.

## The key counter-rule

Never optimize for “less AI-looking” by making everything beige, monochrome, Swiss, minimal, or brutalist.

That is simply another default prior.

Optimize for **specificity**: the interface should look like the product made a decision.
